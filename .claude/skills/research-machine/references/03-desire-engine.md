# Desire engine

Finding the desires a product can honestly serve, ranking them, and choosing the one to lead with. This is the spine of the packet. Avatars and angles fall out of it, not alongside it.

## The governing idea

You cannot create desire. You channel it. Desires already exist in the market as permanent forces; what changes is how people expect them to be satisfied. An ad does not invent a want, it connects an existing want to this product through whatever explanation the market will currently accept.

This has a hard consequence: if the research cannot find the desire already being expressed, the answer is not a cleverer hook. It is that you are pointed at the wrong desire.

## Two source taxonomies

Use both. Most products sit against one from each list.

**The six mass instincts.** What people permanently want.

| Instinct | What it means | Desires it produces |
|---|---|---|
| Health | Survival, safety, staying well | Pain relief, energy, longevity, protection |
| Status | Importance, respect, being ahead | Achievement, recognition, being seen as capable |
| Sex | Attractiveness, being wanted | Looking good, confidence, romantic success |
| Comfort | Ease, avoiding effort and pain | Convenience, simplicity, rest |
| Control | Power, predictability, being in charge | Independence, certainty, mastery |
| Belonging | Fitting in, acceptance, not being rejected | Community, being understood |

**The six technological problems.** What products permanently fail at. Each carries a feeling and an angle, which is what makes this list more useful than the instincts for most products.

| Problem | The feeling it causes | The angle it opens |
|---|---|---|
| Complexity | Feeling stupid | "Simple enough that anyone can" |
| Overwhelm | Anxiety | "One thing instead of five" |
| Fragility | Frustration | "Built to survive actual use" |
| Maintenance | Fatigue | "Set it and stop thinking about it" |
| Incompatibility | Feeling trapped | "Works with what you already have" |
| Obsolescence | Feeling cheated | "Still right in three years" |

## Finding candidate desires

Desires are harvested, not brainstormed. Every candidate desire must arrive with a verbatim quote and a link, or it is not a candidate.

Look for: explicit want statements ("I want", "I wish", "I need"), recurring complaints, workarounds people have cobbled together themselves (the strongest signal in the list, because someone spent effort), failed solutions and why they failed, dream-outcome language, and fear language.

Then run the why-chain on each. Take the purchase in the buyer's own words and ask "why does that matter?" until the answer is an emotion or an identity, then stop. Four to six links is normal. Write every link and label it. The last link is the desire. A pain ladder without its chain is a list; the chain is how the next person reaches the same desire in two minutes.

**The language fidelity check.** Before a desire goes in the packet, search the source corpus for the exact word you used to name it. If nobody used that word, you invented the framing. The worked failure is a researcher writing "hydrated skin" when every reviewer said "smoother and less dry"; the ad built on "hydrated" had nothing to grip. Name desires in the market's words, then say what they mean in yours.

## Ranking: scope, urgency, staying power

Score each candidate desire on three axes. These are judgement calls made against collected evidence, not measurements, so the scale has to be anchored and the uncertainty has to survive into the output.

| Axis | Question | Anchors |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | How many people share it? | 1 = a handful of posts. 5 = recurring across several communities. 9 = a demand proxy confirms it at scale. |
| Urgency | How badly do they want relief? | 1 = mentioned in passing. 5 = they tried something. 9 = they bought, complained or asked for help. |
| Staying power | Does it renew, or is it solved once? | 1 = solved permanently by one purchase. 5 = recurs irregularly. 9 = ongoing or worsening. |

Record the evidence behind each score beside it, and mark any axis you could not evidence as `unknown`. **An axis with no evidence stays `unknown`. It never gets a middling score to keep the arithmetic tidy**, because an invented 5 is indistinguishable from a measured one once it is in a table.

**Weighting is a policy, not a law.** Scope carries the most weight *by default*, because a milder desire held by many usually beats an intense one held by few. That default flips in real situations, and the packet must say which case it is in:

- Early or pre-launch, where the job is a first repeatable sale rather than a ceiling, urgency and reachability matter more than scope. Go where it burns.
- Constrained budget or a narrow initial audience: reachability outranks raw scope.
- A product that honestly serves the intense desire but only partly serves the broad one: fit outranks both.
- Weak economics on the broad desire, for instance high return rates or low margin: contribution outranks scope.

State the weighting used and the reason for it. A ranking with an unstated policy is an opinion wearing a table.

Staying power is allowed to score near zero, and saying so honestly is part of the job. A one-time purchase that solves the problem permanently has a real ceiling, and pretending otherwise produces a retention plan that cannot work.

## Intensity, which sets the price the market will bear

Rank each desire on how hard it burns. Intensity tends to move inversely with price sensitivity, and that tendency is a useful starting hypothesis.

**It is a heuristic, not measured elasticity.** This table has never been calibrated against your market, and several things move willingness to pay independently of how badly someone wants relief: the budget they actually have, how many substitutes exist, whether they trust the category enough to buy from anyone, whether a third party pays, and how quickly they need it. A bleeding-neck desire held by someone with no money converts at zero. Treat the tier as a prediction to test in pricing, never as a licence to raise price because the desire scored high.

| Tier | The felt state | Price sensitivity | What it means commercially |
|---|---|---|---|
| Must-be-nice | "That would be cool" | High | Hard to scale cold; needs a sharp offer or a warm audience |
| Annoyance | "This bugs me, but not today" | Medium to high | Converts on convenience and low friction; rarely premium |
| Keep-me-up-at-night | "I need this fixed soon" | Low | Where the money is; sustains premium pricing |
| Bleeding-neck | "I need this fixed now" | Near zero | First acceptable solution wins; often time-boxed |

Intensity is a different axis from awareness. Awareness is how much the buyer knows. Intensity is how badly they want it. Score both; they fail differently. A clean ad on a must-be-nice desire underperforms because the desire does not burn, and sending that ad for a copy rewrite fixes nothing.

Never manufacture intensity. A desire is only as intense as buyers actually feel it, and dressing an annoyance as an emergency with invented urgency is a claim problem, not a creative choice.

## Four gates before you pick the lead desire

1. **Score.** Scope, urgency and staying power, weighted by the conditional policy in this file's scoring section. There is one weighting policy; do not carry a fixed weighting from memory.
2. **Breadth against stage.** A smaller business should lead with the narrowest credible desire and widen as the product, offer and brand improve. A broad desire needs a strong product and offer to convert, because it competes with everyone. Narrow first, widen with evidence.
3. **Deliverability and believability.** The product must actually satisfy the desire, and the mechanism must be believable enough for that specific desire. This gate kills roughly half of a typical brainstorm and almost nobody writes it down.
4. **Elevation.** See below.

## Elevation, and the move most people miss

The same product can be sold against different instincts. Walk the ladder deliberately rather than defaulting to the category's habit.

Everyone in a category tends to sell the same instinct: health brands sell health, security brands sell control. The non-obvious move is often **downward or sideways** into an instinct nobody in the category is using. A health product sold on comfort or control can find an uncontested lane while every competitor shouts the same word.

Elevation must feel true. Forced elevation reads as manipulation and buyers notice. If the product cannot honestly serve the instinct you elevated to, gate 3 has already failed.

## The demand-pool ceiling

Every desire addresses a finite pool, and a winning angle can scale until that pool saturates. Saturation is one credible cause of a plateau. It is not the only one, and public-conversation research cannot tell you which cause is operating in a particular ad account.

**Rule the packet out of the diagnosis.** Before saturation is proposed, the other candidates have to be visible and unexcluded: creative fatigue, delivery and auction changes, tracking or attribution breakage, a new competitor, a price or promotion change, stock and shipping, the offer itself, and landing-page conversion. Most of those are account-side facts this method never sees. So the packet may say "saturation is consistent with what we found, here is the evidence", and it may hand over the next-ranked desires as a plan. It must not say a plateau *is* saturation, and it must not present desire-switching as the only escape.

At a plateau, the sequence worth trying is:

1. Vary the expression of the same desire: new hooks, formats and angles. Cheap, and it reaches the remainder of the current pool. If this restores growth, the constraint was expression, not pool.
2. If that is exhausted and the account-side causes above have been checked and excluded by whoever owns the account, then a larger or fresher desire is the reasonable next move.

This is why the packet ranks several desires rather than naming one. The second and third ranked desires are the plateau plan, offered as a hypothesis to test rather than a diagnosis.

## What the desire section must contain

For each desire that survives:

- the desire in the market's own words, with a verbatim quote and link
- the why-chain, every link, each labelled
- hell and heaven as concrete scenes, not adjectives: the worst plausible future if nothing changes, and the specific better future, both in the buyer's sensory detail
- scope, urgency and staying power scores with the reasoning
- intensity tier with the evidence for it
- demand proxy and trend direction from search-demand data, or an explicit note that sizing was unavailable. It is a proxy for query volume, never a headcount of buyers, and it is never called pool size
- whether the product can honestly satisfy it, and where it cannot
- the instinct and technological problem it sits against

Then: the ranked list, the one to lead with, the reasoning, and which desire is the plateau plan.
